## The Winding Road
---

I followed a non-linear path in science to arrive where I am at today.

I started off as a geoscience major while studying jazz guitar, switched tracks to enter the sphere of geobiology, honed my skills with bioinformatics, and dabbled in some marine microbiology. Throw in some years of leading bike touring trips as well. 

I've been thrown into the deep end (literally and figuratively) of different fields of research. I've come to appreciate that we learn the most when we are put up to the task of something new and foreign to us. My research typically focuses on a lot of work done in the lab, and primarily a lot of time staring at a computer screen. That is why I find it vital that my research is grounded by conducting field work so that students who become future researches can gain a better understanding and connection to their surroundings, but also better appreciate and respect the environment around them.

As an educator and advisor for students, the classroom should be an incubator for curiosity and creativity while offering a safe environment for students to gain confidence in their identity and belonging in science. I've learned from my bike touring experiences that the classroom (indoor and outdoor) is very very much a two-way learning opportunity. This requires knowing how to facilitate the needs of all of your students, learning to improvise and cater so that everyone can get the same fulfilling experience.

I learned about the microbial world surrounding us by having the privilege to visit all different types of field sites around the globe - from mines, hydrothermal pools, to ophiolites. In particular, I focused on subsurface ecosystems in dynamic and extreme environments 

I had the opportunity during my postdoc to continue exploring subsurface systems, instead directing my efforts towards marine environments.
